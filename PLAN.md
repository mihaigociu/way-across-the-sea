## 1. Vision

An educational browser game. The player opens an **ancient-looking map of the Mediterranean world** (around AD 30–330) and follows Christianity as it spreads, from a small group in Jerusalem to the faith of the Empire. The player:

- **travels** the roads and sea lanes (Paul's journeys especially), with ships sailing across the map;
- **watches churches appear** city by city, as glowing markers spreading over the map;
- **lives through the big events**: Pentecost, the Council of Jerusalem, the fire of Rome, the persecutions, the plagues, the Milvian Bridge, Nicaea;
- **meets the Roman world as it really was**: its roads, baths, trade and law, and also its slavery, arena, infant exposure and crucifixion;
- **understands *why*** the message appealed to slaves, women, the poor, the sick, Jews and Greeks;
- **passes a quiz** at the end of each chapter to unlock the next.

**One sentence:** *"Google Earth meets a storybook meets a quiz game, set on a map that looks like a 2,000-year-old scroll."*

### Learning goals (what the player should leave knowing)
1. The geography: Mediterranean, provinces, the main cities and ports, and how people travelled (roads, ships, sailing seasons).
2. The timeline, from roughly 30 to 330 (optionally to 380), and its main turning points.
3. The key people: Peter, Paul, Barnabas, Stephen, Lydia, Priscilla & Aquila, Phoebe, Timothy, Ignatius, Polycarp, Perpetua & Felicity, Cyprian, Constantine, and others.
4. What daily life was like in the Roman world, for both the privileged and the powerless.
5. **Why Christianity grew**: the social, moral and spiritual reasons, not just "it happened."
6. How historians know these things: sources (Acts, the Epistles, Pliny, Tacitus, Eusebius, archaeology), and the difference between **history, early tradition, and later legend**.

## 2. Audience & tone: ages 10–14

**Writing rules for this age:**
- Story cards of **at most ~60–80 words**. One idea per card. Lots of pictures, few dates (only the key ones).
- Vocabulary explained in context. Hard words (*synagogue, epistle, martyr, province*) get a tap-for-definition tooltip that saves the word to the Codex.
- An optional **"Want to know more?"** fold-out on each card, for curious kids, parents and teachers. This keeps some depth without a separate "Scholar" mode.
- **Cruelty is implied, never shown:** crosses seen far away on a hill; the arena as an empty stadium with crowd noise and the companion's worried face; the plague as a dark fog over the map. No blood, no bodies, no torture scenes. A short **note for parents and group leaders** in each chapter suggests how to talk about it.
- **Hopeful, curious tone:** the companion asks the questions a child would ask ("Why would anyone *want* to watch that?", "Why do they share their bread with strangers?").
- **Told from inside the faith:** the narrators are early Christians, and they tell what they saw and believe in their own words: *"Jesus had risen from the dead! Peter and the others saw him alive."*, *"God healed a man who had never walked."* There is no "Christians believe…" or "Acts tells…" in the story text. The **"Want to know more?"** fold-outs and the leader's guide add the historian's notes (sources, dates, what is tradition) for curious kids and adults.

**Languages:** English + **Romanian** from day one (i18n built in). That fits the Romanian audience (school / parish / *Școala Christiana*), and it gives us a lovely local hook: **St. Andrew in Scythia Minor (Dobrogea)**, presented as early tradition, plus the **martyrs of Tomis / Noviodunum / Durostorum**, Epictetus & Astion of Halmyris, and the 4th-century Christian basilicas of Dobrogea.

**Tone principles:**
- **Warm and faithful, but historically grounded:** places, dates, customs and people are accurate. Where something comes from later tradition (e.g. where the apostles went), the narrators say so naturally (*"some of this we only heard from travellers"*), and the fold-out explains it.
- **Show cruelty with purpose**, never for shock. Each dark element answers the question *"what did this world look like to the vulnerable, and what did the Christian message offer them?"*
- **Avoid anti-Jewish framing.** Christianity starts *inside* Judaism. Synagogues of the diaspora were the first "network." The Jewish people suffered too (AD 70, AD 135).
- Romans aren't cartoon villains either. Roman roads, peace, law and the Greek language are *why* the message could travel at all.

## 2b. The companion: a seafaring family across 300 years

**The problem:** one child can't live from AD 30 to 330.
**The idea:** the player follows **one family of sailors**, whose children grow up and pass the story to the next generation. Two objects tie it together:

- 🪔 **The family lamp**: a small clay oil lamp from the family ship. It's the game's logo, and it's the "lamp" that lights up on the map when a church is founded.
- 📜 **The family logbook**: a codex the children write in and hand down. **It *is* the in-game Codex** (people, places, words, scrolls collected). The player literally inherits it chapter after chapter.

The family are **ship people** (the *Way Across the Sea*). The father is a *kubernetes*, the ship's pilot of the merchant ship ***Euploia*** ("Good Sailing"), based in Caesarea. That's why they're in every port, and why sailing, trade and weather matter in the story.

The family are **believers**. Grandfather Eli was baptized at Pentecost, and the children grow up in the faith. Their mother is Jewish and their father Greek, so the kids still meet every kind of person and ask honest questions ("Why do some people hate us?", "Why does Paul go back to the town that attacked him?"). Their faith is lived, not lectured.

| Generation | Chapters | Companion(s) | Hook |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grandfather Eli** (as a story, told in flashback) | Ch. 1 | The children's Jewish grandfather (their mother's father). He was about 50 at Pentecost (AD 30) and is in his mid-60s in AD 46; their mother was then a young unmarried woman | "I was there when they spoke in every language…" |
| **Nikias (12) & Thalia (11)**, brother and sister | Ch. 2–6 | Grow up from children (~AD 46) to young adults (~AD 64) | They meet Paul at Antioch, sail with Lydia's purple-cloth cargo, survive the Malta shipwreck as young crew (the "action chapter") |
| **Nikias's grandchildren** | Ch. 7 | **Dorotheos** (11), in Smyrna | Hears old Polycarp; the logbook is now "old" |
| **A great-granddaughter** | Ch. 8 | **Felicitas "Feli"** (12), in Carthage | Plague of Cyprian; a choice to stay and help |
| **The last link** | Ch. 9 | **Constantius "Costa"** (13), apprentice scribe to a bishop | Travels to Nicaea; the lamp arrives at the council |
| **Bonus** | Ch. ★ | **Ana** (12), a cousin's line settled in Tomis | The Black Sea branch of the family |

Notes:
- **Fictional characters only.** They meet real people (Paul, Lydia, Priscilla…), but never put words in their mouths beyond what the sources say or what closely paraphrases them.
- **The player follows both siblings together.** Nikias and Thalia are a duo with different personalities, which drives the dialogue. *Nikias* is bold, loves ships and wants to be a pilot like his father. *Thalia* is thoughtful, asks "why?", and notices people others ignore.
- **Thalia's foundling thread (confirmed).** It runs gently across Chapters 2–6:
  - **Ch. 2**: small hints. Thalia doesn't look like the rest of the family, and an old dock-worker at Caesarea gives her a strange look.
  - **Ch. 3**: in Philippi she meets a slave girl her own age and wonders, "what if that were me?"
  - **Ch. 4**: her mother tells her the truth. As a baby she was left outside the city (Roman parents were allowed to do this). Her father heard her crying on the way to the harbour and carried her home. *"You weren't found by chance. You were chosen."*
  - **Ch. 5–6**: Thalia sees Christian families doing the same for other babies, and understands why this mattered so much. Later, as a grown-up, she takes in a foundling herself, which starts the next generation of the family.
  - *Tone: tender and safe. No details of the abandonment beyond "left outside the city". A parent/leader note helps adults handle questions (e.g. for adopted children in the group).*
- A **family tree screen** fills in as generations pass. It shows time passing in a way kids can feel.

## 3. Look & feel

**Art direction: "living ancient map."**
- Parchment/papyrus texture, sepia ink, hand-drawn coastlines, faded colour washes for provinces.
- Inspirations: the **Tabula Peutingeriana** (Roman road map), Ptolemy's *Geographia* maps, medieval portolan charts, Byzantine mosaics (for character portraits and the UI frame).
- Latin/Greek labels with modern names on hover: *Antiochia (Antakya)*, *Ephesus (near Selçuk)*, *Tomis (Constanța)*.
- **Animated life on the map**: small ships with sails moving along sea lanes, wind and wave strokes, caravans on roads, smoke from Rome in AD 64, and storm clouds for Paul's shipwreck.
- **Spreading light**: each Christian community appears as a small lamp (an oil lamp with a chi-rho or fish). By AD 300 the map glows.
- Typography: a Roman-capitals display font (e.g. *Cinzel*), plus a highly readable body font (*Atkinson Hyperlegible*, as on the physics site).
- Sound (optional, later): lyre/oud ambient music, waves, harbor noise, a short chant at key moments.

**Screens:**
1. **Title**: an unrolling scroll, a ship sailing past the lighthouse of Alexandria.
2. **World map**: the main hub, with zoom, pan and time slider.
3. **City view**: an illustrated card or panorama of a city (Ephesus theatre, Corinth agora, Rome forum), with people to talk to and "discoveries" to find.
4. **Journey mode**: the player plots or follows a route, and the ship or traveller moves along it while events trigger.
5. **Story scene**: a comic/visual-novel style dialogue with characters.
6. **Codex**: an encyclopedia that unlocks as you play (people, places, terms, sources).
7. **Quiz gate**: at the end of each chapter.
8. **Timeline**: a horizontal scroll of everything unlocked.
9. **Family tree**: the companion generations.

## 3b. Art pipeline: AI-generated + public domain (for now)

- **Public domain first** for anything "real": the Tabula Peutingeriana, catacomb frescoes, Dura-Europos paintings, mosaics, coins, inscriptions, and photos of ruins (Wikimedia Commons, museums with open-access collections: the Met, the Rijksmuseum, the British Museum where licence allows).
- **AI-generated** for the illustrated parts: the companion family, city panoramas, story scenes, ships, and UI ornaments.
- **One style guide + a prompt library** (`art/STYLE.md`, `art/prompts/`) so every AI image matches: e.g. *"warm sepia ink and watercolour on parchment, soft lines, children's-book illustration, Byzantine-mosaic colour accents"*. Fixed **character sheets** (reference images) for each companion keep faces consistent.
- **Review every AI image for anachronisms** (no stirrups, no medieval castles, no modern clothes, correct ships: a *corbita* merchant ship with a square sail and a steering oar, not a galleon) and for respectful depiction of real figures (Paul, Peter: following traditional iconography loosely, not inventing).
- **Asset IDs, not file names, in the content** (`img: scene.philippi.lydia`). An illustrator can later replace the images one by one without touching the story files.
- **`art/CREDITS.yaml`**: source, licence and author/tool for every image, plus a note in the game's "About" page that some images are AI-generated.

## 4. Game structure: chapters (levels)

Each chapter = **map phase** (travel and discover) → **story scenes** → **"Life in the Empire" vignette** → **quiz gate**.

### Prologue: *Mare Nostrum* (c. AD 30)
- Tour of the Empire under Tiberius: Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, Ephesus, Corinth, Carthage, Jerusalem.
- Mechanics tutorial: pan/zoom, the roads (*viae*), the sea lanes, the **sailing season** (*mare clausum*: no safe sailing roughly Nov–Mar).
- The "ingredients" that made the spread possible: **Pax Romana, roads, Greek as a common language, Jewish diaspora synagogues in every big city.**
- Vignette: *a day in a Roman city* (baths, forum, temples, slaves everywhere).

### Chapter 1: Jerusalem (c. 30–35)
- Crucifixion under Pontius Pilate, told gently: three crosses far away on a hill, and Grandfather Eli remembers how frightened everyone was. (Explained: Rome's punishment for slaves and rebels, meant to terrify.)
- Resurrection proclaimed, **Pentecost**: pilgrims "from every nation" (Acts 2). Lines on the map go out to their home regions.
- The first community: shared goods, care for widows, the deacons.
- **Stephen**, the first martyr; the community scatters; **Philip** in Samaria and with the Ethiopian official (a line toward Africa).
- **Saul of Tarsus** on the road to Damascus.

### Chapter 2: Antioch, and the first journey (c. 40–48)
- Antioch, the 3rd city of the Empire: here the disciples are first called *Christianoi*.
- **Peter & Cornelius** (the first gentile convert, a Roman centurion, at Caesarea).
- **Paul & Barnabas, first journey**: Seleucia → Cyprus (Salamis, Paphos) → Perga → Pisidian Antioch → Iconium → Lystra → Derbe → back.
- Event: at Lystra they're hailed as Zeus and Hermes, then stoned.
- **Council of Jerusalem** (c. 49): do gentiles need to become Jews first? (a decision mini-game: the player hears the arguments.)

### Chapter 3: Into Europe, the second journey (c. 49–52)
- Antioch → Galatia → **Troas** → the "Macedonian vision" (the player *chooses* to cross to Europe).
- **Philippi**: Lydia, the purple-cloth merchant (the first convert in Europe); the slave girl; Paul and Silas in prison, then the earthquake.
- **Thessalonica**, **Berea**, then **Athens** (the Areopagus speech to the "unknown god", and meeting the philosophers: Stoics and Epicureans).
- **Corinth**, 18 months: **Priscilla & Aquila** (tentmakers expelled from Rome by Claudius); the Gallio inscription, a rare *hard date*.
- Letters begin: 1 Thessalonians becomes a collectible **scroll**.

### Chapter 4: Ephesus, the third journey (c. 53–57)
- Ephesus: the temple of **Artemis** (one of the Seven Wonders), magic books burned, the **riot of the silversmiths** in the great theatre.
- Letters: 1 & 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Romans (with Phoebe of Cenchreae carrying it to Rome).
- The collection for the poor of Jerusalem: an early "charity network" across provinces.
- Farewell at **Miletus**; arrest in Jerusalem; two years in prison at Caesarea; Paul appeals to Caesar as a Roman citizen.

### Chapter 5: The voyage to Rome (c. 59–60), the "action chapter"
- A **sailing mini-game** on a real route: Caesarea → Sidon → Myra (grain ship from Alexandria) → Cnidus → Fair Havens (Crete).
- The player is warned it's too late in the year (*the Fast*, autumn). Sailing anyway brings the **Euraquilo storm**: 14 days adrift, throwing cargo overboard, **shipwreck on Malta** (the viper, healing Publius' father).
- Then Syracuse → Rhegium → **Puteoli** → the **Via Appia** (Forum of Appius, Three Taverns) → **Rome**.
- Gameplay lesson: real Roman navigation, the grain trade, and the sailing season.

### Chapter 6: Fire and sword (64–70)
- **Great Fire of Rome** (64); **Nero** blames the Christians (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44, quoted). Christians are burned as torches and thrown to beasts.
- Martyrdom of **Peter** (Vatican Hill) and **Paul** (Via Ostiensis), per early tradition.
- **Jewish revolt; destruction of Jerusalem & the Temple (70)**, and the Arch of Titus.
- The Gospels are written (the dates are discussed carefully).
- Vignette: *the arena*. Nikias goes to the games with a friend. We see the crowd, hear the noise, and see his face as he slowly understands what is happening. Then he leaves. Nothing is shown directly.

### Chapter 7: The quiet growth (c. 70–200)
- The church spreads without a "hero journey": **through households, trade networks, slaves and wives**.
- **Ignatius of Antioch** writes letters on his way to the beasts in Rome (c. 107–110): a route on the map.
- **Pliny the Younger to Emperor Trajan** (c. 112): "what do I do with these Christians?" The player reads the real letter and Trajan's reply.
- **Polycarp of Smyrna** martyred (c. 155): *"Eighty-six years I have served Him…"*
- **Justin Martyr** in Rome; the **apologists** argue that Christians aren't cannibals or atheists (the rumours explained).
- **Martyrs of Lyon & Vienne** (177): Blandina, a slave girl.
- **Antonine Plague** (165–180): the first big plague mechanic (see §5).
- Lamps appear in Gaul, North Africa, Egypt, Mesopotamia (Edessa).

### Chapter 8: The century of crisis (c. 200–284)
- **Perpetua & Felicity** (Carthage, 203): a noblewoman and her slave die side by side. Her diary is among the earliest surviving writings by a Christian woman.
- **Catacombs** of Rome; early Christian art (fish, anchor, Good Shepherd).
- **Dura-Europos house church** (c. 240): the oldest known church building. An explore-the-building scene.
- **Plague of Cyprian** (c. 250–270): pagans flee the cities while Christians stay to nurse the sick, pagans included (Cyprian, Dionysius of Alexandria).
- **Decius' persecution** (250): the *libellus* certificate of sacrifice. Feli watches a neighbour decide whether to sign it. Then the debate about the *lapsi* who gave in.
- **Valerian** (257–258): Cyprian executed.
- Crisis of the 3rd century: inflation, civil wars, invasions. The Empire is falling apart, but the church is organized and resilient.
- **Armenia** becomes the first Christian kingdom (traditionally 301).

### Chapter 9: The Great Persecution and the turn (303–330)
- **Diocletian's Great Persecution** (303–311): scriptures burned, churches destroyed. Costa's bishop hides the church's books. (The martyrs of Dobrogea get their own bonus chapter ★.)
- **Edict of Galerius** (311).
- **Battle of the Milvian Bridge** (312): Constantine's sign.
- **Edict of Milan** (313).
- **Council of Nicaea** (325): bishops from all over the map gather. Every lamp from the game "travels" to Nicaea, a visual finale.
- **Constantinople founded** (330): **the end of the game.** Costa, now grown, lights the old family lamp in the new city, and the whole map glows. Final reflection: *"300 years ago, a few frightened people in Jerusalem. Now…"* A teaser points to a possible sequel (the *byzantium* project).

### Chapter ★ (bonus): *By the Black Sea*, Scythia Minor (Dobrogea)
Unlocks after Chapter 5 (Andrew's part) and is completed after Chapter 9 (the martyrs' part). Follows **Ana**, from the family's Black Sea branch in **Tomis** (Constanța).
- **The Greek colonies** on the Black Sea (*Pontus Euxinus*): Tomis, Histria, Callatis. A fun fact: the poet **Ovid** was exiled to Tomis and complained about the cold!
- **St. Andrew in Scythia**: an *early tradition* (Origen, quoted by Eusebius, says Andrew's mission was "Scythia"). Shown as a tradition, clearly labelled ("Christians in Dobrogea have long told that…"). Includes the "Cave of St. Andrew" as a place of local memory.
- The Roman **Danube frontier** (*limes*): forts at Noviodunum (Isaccea), Halmyris (Murighiol), Durostorum (Silistra), and the soldiers who brought new beliefs with them.
- **The martyrs of Dobrogea** under Diocletian and his co-emperors (c. 290–304), e.g. **Epictetus & Astion** at Halmyris, **Dasius** at Durostorum, and the martyrs of **Niculițel** (Zoticus, Attalus, Kamasis, Philippus). Their **crypt was rediscovered in 1971**, and an "archaeologist" mini-scene lets the player uncover it. All told gently, with no scenes of violence.
- Early **basilicas** of Tomis, Histria, Tropaeum Traiani (Adamclisi), and the **Tropaeum Traiani monument** itself (from Trajan's Dacian wars).
- The ending links to Nicaea: a bishop from Scythia travels there *(the exact attendee lists need checking with a historian)*.
- *Sources to verify with an expert: Eusebius HE 3.1; the Passions of Epictetus & Astion, of Dasius; the Niculițel excavation reports.*

## 5. Core mechanics

### 5.1 The map
- Pan/zoom over an illustrated Mediterranean (Britain → Mesopotamia, Rhine/Danube → Egypt/Africa).
- **Time slider** (AD 30 → 330): lamps appear and the borders of persecution change.
- **Layers** you can toggle: roads, sea lanes, provinces, Jewish diaspora communities, Christian communities, persecutions, plagues.
- **Cities** as clickable markers, with size by population (Rome ~1M, Alexandria ~500k, Antioch ~250k, Ephesus ~200k…).

### 5.2 Travel
- Routes along real Roman roads and sea lanes. Travel times based on **ORBIS** (Stanford's model of the Roman transport network). E.g. "Rome → Alexandria: ~2 weeks by sea in summer, impossible in winter."
- A **season system**: sailing in winter is risky (Chapter 5 teaches this the hard way).
- Choose **by land vs. by sea**: the cost/time/danger trade-off (bandits, storms, pirates).

### 5.3 "The Network" (spread meter)
- Each city has a small panel: *synagogue? Christian community? size? leader? persecuted?*
- A simple **spread model**, loosely based on sociologist Rodney Stark's estimates (~1,000 Christians in 40 → ~6 million by 300, about 40% growth per decade). The player sees the numbers grow on a chart and learns *how* compound growth works. (This could connect to the physics/maths projects too.)
- What drives growth in the model: **trade links, Jewish communities, care during plagues, households, women's networks, martyr testimony.**

### 5.4 Plague mechanic (Chapters 7–8)
- The plague spreads across the map like a dark fog.
- The player sees two kinds of city behaviour: **flight** (the wealthy and the doctors leave; the sick are abandoned) vs. **staying** (Christian communities nurse the sick).
- Result: more survivors among those cared for, new bonds and conversions. This is grounded in Cyprian and Dionysius, and explained with simple numbers (basic nursing like water, food and warmth can cut mortality a lot).

### 5.5 Personas: "Through their eyes"
The heart of the *"why did people join?"* question. These are short side-stories (3–5 cards each) in which the **companion meets someone** and spends a day with them. Everything is written for ages 10–14: the hard realities are named simply, never shown.

| Who the companion meets | Their life in the Roman world | Why the Christian groups appealed to them |
|---|---|---|
| **Onesima**, a household slave girl (Corinth) | She "belongs" to a master, can be sold, and has no say over her own life | At the evening meal she's called "sister" and eats at the same table as free people |
| **Lydia's workers** (Philippi) | Hard work in the dye shops | A woman leader who hosts the community in her house |
| **A widow** (Jerusalem / Antioch) | No husband often meant no money and no protection | The community brought her food every day |
| **Thalia herself** (the foundling thread, optional) | Unwanted babies were sometimes left outside to die. It was legal and common | Christians were known for taking in abandoned babies |
| **A family in an *insula*** (Antioch) | A crowded, dark apartment; fires and illness | Neighbours who visit the sick and share bread |
| **A young legionary** (Caesarea) | Strict army life; must honour the emperor as a god | A different kind of "Lord", one who serves |
| **A God-fearer** (synagogue at Pisidian Antioch) | A gentile who loves the One God but can't fully belong | Full belonging, without having to become Jewish first |
| **A philosophy student** (Athens) | Clever ideas, but little hope about what happens after death | A message about love, meaning and resurrection |
| **The arena crowd** (Rome) | Fights to the death as entertainment | The belief that every human life is precious |

Each side-story ends with a reflection card, *"Why did Onesima want to join us?"*, told in the children's own voice: what they saw the community do for her.
### 5.6 Collectibles
- **Scrolls**: Paul's letters (each one is linked to its city, with a short excerpt and a plain-language summary).
- **Artefacts**: the Gallio inscription, the *libellus* of Decius, the Alexamenos graffito (the earliest mocking image of Christian worship), the ichthys fish, catacomb frescoes, chi-rho coins of Constantine.
- **People cards**: real figures, illustrated in a Byzantine-mosaic style.
- All collected items go into the **family logbook** (the Codex).

### 5.7 Decision moments (light choices, no "wrong history")
- "Cross to Macedonia?" "Sail from Fair Havens before winter?" "Stay in the plague city or flee?" (Feli, Ch. 8) "Hide the church books or hand them over?" (Costa, Ch. 9)
- The *libellus* decision is told through an adult's choice that the companion witnesses, not one the child makes, because it fits the age better.
- History happens the way it did. Choices change *what you learn and see*, not the outcome. After each choice: *"Here's what actually happened, and why."*

## 6. Quizzes (level gates)

- **Passing score**: ~70%, with retries allowed. Wrong answers show the explanation and a link to the relevant Codex entry.
- **Question types** (mixed, to keep it fun):
  1. **Multiple choice**: "Who was the first convert in Europe?"
  2. **Map drop**: drag "Corinth" to the right spot on the map.
  3. **Route tracing**: draw Paul's 1st journey in order.
  4. **Timeline ordering**: put Pentecost / Council of Jerusalem / Fire of Rome / Nicaea in order.
  5. **Who said it?**: match simple quotes to Paul, Pliny, Polycarp.
  6. **Letter-to-city matching**: Galatians ↔ Galatia, Philippians ↔ Philippi…
  7. **"Why?" questions**: "Why did Christians stay in cities during plagues?" (to test understanding, not just facts).
  8. **"How do we know?"**: is this from a written source, from archaeology, or a tradition? (teaches source criticism, kid-style).
- **~8–10 questions per gate**, mostly visual (map, pictures, ordering); short text; friendly feedback from the companion ("Almost! Lydia sold *purple* cloth, remember?").
- **Sailor ranks** (fits the theme): *Ship's child → Deckhand → Sailor → Navigator → Pilot (Kubernetes)*. Stars per chapter; the rank shows on the family tree.
- **Group mode**: see §6b.

## 6b. At home and in parish groups

The game is played in two settings, and the design supports both:

**Session length:** each chapter is split into **2–3 "episodes" of about 15–20 minutes**, and each episode ends at a natural stopping point (arriving in a port). The full game is about 25–30 episodes, roughly **one school year of weekly parish meetings**, or a few weeks at home.

**At home (solo or with a parent):**
- Progress is saved automatically (localStorage). A **"Continue the journey"** button shows where the ship stopped.
- **"Ask a grown-up" cards** at sensitive moments (e.g. the foundling reveal or the arena) suggest talking it over together.
- Works on a tablet or laptop. No account, no ads, no tracking, no data collection.

**In a parish group (led by a catechist or youth leader):**
- **Group mode**: big text and buttons for a **projector or TV**. The leader drives, and kids vote on choices by show of hands ("Do we sail from Fair Havens or wait for spring?").
- **Team quiz**: the quiz gate can be played in 2–4 teams, with points on screen.
- A **Leader's guide** per chapter (printable PDF/page) with:
  - a summary and the learning goals;
  - **3–5 discussion questions** (e.g. *"Why did people share their bread with strangers? Who would we call a stranger today?"*);
  - a short **hands-on activity**: draw your own fish symbol, write a letter like Paul, a map-drawing challenge, or make a clay-lamp craft;
  - the **sensitive moments** in the chapter and how to handle them;
  - the printable quiz and answer sheet, plus a printable map;
  - suggested Bible and source passages (Acts, the letters, Pliny…), with short historical notes.
- **Printable extras**: a big wall map that the group fills with lamp stickers as the weeks go on, and **companion cards** for each character.

## 7. Content: the Roman world, honestly (for kids)

Short **"Life in the Empire"** pages, one per chapter (illustrated, 1–2 screens each):

**Wonders and particularities:** roads and milestones · aqueducts & baths · the grain fleet & Ostia · insulae (apartment blocks) · the forum & law courts · Roman citizenship (Paul's trump card) · patrons & clients · household gods & the emperor cult · mystery religions (Isis, Mithras, Cybele) · philosophy schools · Koine Greek · the synagogue network · letters & couriers · the Jewish diaspora.

**The hard side (named simply, implied rather than shown, with a parent/leader note each):** slavery (~10–20% of people in Italy, maybe more in some cities) · crucifixion · the arena · abandoned babies · a father's total power over his family · girls married very young · poverty, disease, and many children dying young · plagues · the persecution of Christians · the destruction of Jerusalem.

**Why did people join?** This is a summary panel that unlocks progressively, in the family's voice (*"this is why so many came to us"*). The leader's guide links each reason to the historians behind it: Rodney Stark, *The Rise of Christianity*; Larry Hurtado, *Destroyer of the Gods*; Tom Holland, *Dominion*; Kyle Harper, *The Fate of Rome*; and the ancient sources):
1. **Every person matters**: Christians taught that every person is made in God's image: slave, woman, child, foreigner.
2. **A real community**: a new "family" in anonymous, overcrowded cities.
3. **Concrete charity**: widows, orphans, the sick, prisoners, and burying the dead, even outsiders' dead (Emperor Julian complains about this later!).
4. **Courage under plagues**: staying and nursing.
5. **Better life for women**: marriage norms, no infanticide or forced abortion, and leadership in house churches. The early church was disproportionately female.
6. **Moral clarity & transformation**: a demanding way of life, not just rituals.
7. **Hope beyond death**: resurrection, in a world where death was everywhere.
8. **A personal, loving God**, in contrast to the unpredictable gods of Greek myth and the emperor cult.
9. **Martyr testimony**: "see how they love one another" / "the blood of martyrs is seed."
10. **Practical networks**: synagogues, trade routes, households, letters.

- **DARE** (Digital Atlas of the Roman Empire): Roman roads, cities, provinces.
